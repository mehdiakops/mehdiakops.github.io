Case Study · Motive

Moving an entire knowledge base to a new CMS without losing the traffic

Role
Technical Writer & SEO Lead
Scope
Full knowledge base, 2,000+ articles
Partners
Engineering, Support, Marketing SEO
Outcome
Minimal organic traffic loss

The problem

Our knowledge base had grown into one of the highest-traffic properties Motive owned. A large share of the people reading it had arrived from a search engine, not from inside the product. That made the knowledge base a support asset and an acquisition asset at the same time, and it made the planned CMS change riskier than it looked on the project board.

CMS migrations fail in a specific and predictable way. URLs change, redirects get missed or misconfigured, crawlers find broken paths, rankings slip, and the traffic decline shows up six weeks later when everyone has moved on to the next project. By then the cause is hard to isolate and the recovery is slow.

What constrained the work

  • The migration date was set by the platform contract, not by content readiness.
  • The URL structure on the new CMS was not a one-to-one match with the old one, so redirects could not be generated mechanically.
  • Support agents linked to specific articles inside live customer tickets, so broken links would surface as customer-facing failures immediately, not just as an SEO metric.
  • Regular publishing could not stop. Product releases kept shipping throughout.
Check this list. These are the constraints that typically apply to a migration of this shape. Cut anything that was not true for you and add the one that actually kept you up at night, because that is the detail an interviewer will follow up on.

What I did

Mapped before migrating. I built a complete inventory of the existing knowledge base and mapped every URL to its destination on the new platform before any content moved. Articles being consolidated or retired needed a deliberate decision about where their traffic should land, rather than a default redirect to the homepage, which passes almost no value and frustrates the reader.

Prioritised by traffic, not by alphabet. Not every article carried the same risk. I ranked the inventory by organic sessions and inbound link volume so the highest-value pages got individually verified mapping while the long tail could be handled in patterns.

Partnered with Engineering on the redirect layer. The mapping was mine, the implementation was theirs, and the handoff format mattered. I delivered redirects in a structure Engineering could apply directly rather than a document they had to interpret.

Built QA into launch, not after it. We verified redirect behaviour, canonical tags, sitemap generation and internal link integrity as launch criteria rather than as post-launch cleanup.

Watched the recovery curve. Organic performance after a migration always dips before it settles. I tracked indexation and traffic through that window so we could tell a normal re-crawl dip apart from an actual problem, and fix the real issues quickly.

What happened

The knowledge base moved to the new CMS with minimal organic traffic loss. Support agents kept their existing article links working. Publishing continued through the migration window without a freeze.

What I would do differently

The mapping work was sound. The thing I would change is when it started. I built the inventory once the migration was already scheduled, which meant the retire-or-consolidate decisions were made under time pressure. A content audit that runs continuously, rather than one triggered by a platform change, would have meant arriving at the migration with a smaller and cleaner base to move.

I would also have pushed harder for a staging environment with a representative content sample earlier in the process. Several structural issues were discoverable well before launch week.
